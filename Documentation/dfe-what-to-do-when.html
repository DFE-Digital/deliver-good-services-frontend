Product manager (lead), Delivery manager
Problem statement · Stakeholder map
What

Define the broad area of concern: who is affected, what is going wrong, and what policy or user need is driving this. A concise framing exercise, not a research project.

Why it matters

Without a shared problem framing, Discovery teams can spend weeks on the wrong question. This step is cheap; skipping it isn't.

How
  1. Desk research — reports, policy docs, complaints data
  2. 3–5 informal stakeholder conversations
  3. Write a 1-page problem framing statement
  4. Map who will need to be involved in Discovery

Discovery

Understand users and their needs. Identify constraints, map the landscape, decide whether and how a service should be built.

8–12 weeks · 4–6 people
Activity

Agile
Conduct user research
From week 1 — ongoing throughout Discovery
User researcher (lead), Interaction designer, Product manager
Research plan · Findings report · User needs statements
What

Qualitative research with real users and non-users to understand goals, pain points, mental models and context. Must include diverse participants and people with access needs.

Why it matters

Services that don't understand user needs fail. This is your primary tool for deciding what to build — or whether to build at all. Directly evidences Service Standard point 1.

How
  1. Write a research plan: questions, methods, recruitment criteria
  2. Recruit 8–10+ participants including those with access needs
  3. Run semi-structured interviews or contextual enquiry
  4. Run a synthesis workshop — identify themes, needs and insights
  5. Share findings with the whole team, not just the researcher

8–12 weeks · 5–8 people

Create and test prototypes
Core activity — iterative throughout Alpha sprints
Interaction designer (lead), User researcher, Content designer, Developer
Prototype (iterated) · Usability test findings · Design decision log
What

Quick, throwaway prototypes to test whether design concepts work for real users. Fast experiments to validate or kill assumptions before investing in a full build.

Why it matters

Failing fast in Alpha is cheap. Testing assumptions here prevents expensive redesigns in Beta. Each round should either confirm a decision or change it.

How
  1. Identify the riskiest assumptions from Discovery first
  2. Build with GOV.UK Prototype Kit or Figma — whichever is faster
  3. Run usability testing with at least 5 participants per round
  4. Synthesise findings, update the design, iterate next sprint

Architecture Decision Records · Technical options paper
What

Time-boxed experiments to explore viable technical approaches: hosting, data models, integration patterns, build vs buy decisions before the Beta build begins.

Why it matters

Committing to an architecture in Beta without spiking leads to expensive rework. A spike costs one sprint. Rework costs months.

How
  1. List the top 3–5 technical unknowns from the Discovery constraints map
  2. Time-box each spike to a maximum of one sprint
  3. Document findings as Architecture Decision Records
  4. Produce a technical options paper for DfE architecture review

Sprint 1 — before any feature work begins
Developer (lead), Technical architect
CI/CD pipeline config · Environment docs · Deployment runbook
What

An automated build, test and deployment pipeline enabling every code change to be verified and deployed safely to dev, staging and production environments.

Why it matters

Manual deployments slow teams, introduce errors and make safe iteration impossible. A pipeline from day one lets the team focus on features, not deployments.

How
  1. Set up GitHub Actions or Azure DevOps with build, test, deploy stages
  2. Create dev, staging and production environments with access controls
  3. Require all automated tests to pass before merging to main
  4. Document pipeline configuration and environment structure

Continuous accessibility testing
Every sprint — built into the definition of done
Interaction designer (lead), User researcher, Developer, Content designer
WCAG audit report · Accessibility statement · Known issues log
What

Automated and manual accessibility checks against WCAG 2.2 AA on every component or flow — part of the sprint workflow, not bolted on at the end.

Why it matters

A legal requirement under PSBAR. Retrofitting accessibility is significantly more expensive than building it in. All users must be able to access government services.

How
  1. Integrate axe-core into the CI pipeline for automated checks on every PR
  2. Test manually with NVDA/JAWS on Windows, VoiceOver on iOS each sprint
  3. Include users with access needs in regular usability research rounds
  4. Book a full WCAG audit before public Beta launch
  5. Publish an accessibility statement from day one of public Beta

Service operations (lead), Product manager, Service designer, Delivery manager
Support model document · SLA definition · Tier response runbook
What

Define how users get help: helpdesk channels, self-service, escalation paths, out-of-hours cover and SLA commitments across all severity levels.

Why it matters

A service without adequate support erodes trust. The support model must be in place and tested before the service is exposed to the public.

How
  1. Run a service blueprint workshop with ops, service desk and product
  2. Define tier-1 (self-service), tier-2 (helpdesk) and tier-3 (team) processes
  3. Agree SLAs and escalation criteria with the product owner
  4. Test the support model with real users during private Beta

Service operations (lead), Performance analyst
Monitoring dashboard · Alert runbook · Monthly performance report
What

Real-time monitoring of uptime, error rates, response times, journey completion and support ticket volumes — with defined alerting thresholds.

Why it matters

Problems in Live can affect thousands of users in minutes. Fast detection means fast resolution. Without monitoring, issues go undetected.

How
  1. Set up dashboards covering the 4 service KPIs (Grafana, Azure Monitor)
  2. Define alert thresholds with on-call escalation paths
  3. Publish a public-facing performance dashboard — required for Live assessment
  4. Review monitoring coverage after every significant incident

Incident management
When an incident occurs — follow the defined playbook
Service operations (lead), Developer, Product manager
Incident log · Status communications · Post-incident review
What

A structured process for detecting, classifying, resolving and learning from service incidents — P1 (critical) through to P4 (minor).

Why it matters

Without clear ownership and process, incidents drag on, communications fail and the same issues recur. A defined process reduces time-to-resolution.

How
  1. Assign an incident lead immediately — they own comms and coordination
  2. Classify severity (P1–P4) and follow the appropriate SLA
  3. Communicate status to stakeholders proactively via the status page
  4. Schedule a post-incident review within 5 working days of resolution

What

Review analytics, user feedback, support tickets and survey data to identify where users are struggling, then design improvements based on evidence.

Why it matters

Meeting user needs is an ongoing commitment. Services that aren't actively improved degrade as needs, policy and technology evolve.

How
  1. Monthly analytics review — completion rates, drop-off, error patterns

  3. Maintain a prioritised improvement backlog with evidence links

Annual service review
Once per year, or when context changes significantly
Product manager (lead), Delivery manager, Technical architect, Service operations
Annual review report · Decision record · Next-year roadmap
What

A formal annual review against current user needs, policy objectives, technology health, accessibility compliance and total cost of ownership.

Why it matters

Prevents technical debt accumulation and ensures continued fitness for purpose. Identifies when a new Beta cycle is needed — or when the service should be retired.

How
  1. Use the DfE annual review template across all review dimensions
  2. Include performance data, incident history and user satisfaction scores
  3. Present findings and a recommendation to SLT: continue, rebuild or retire
